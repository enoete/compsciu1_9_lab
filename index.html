CS UNIT 1 · CLASSWORK 9

Pseudocode & flowcharts: a working session

Six stations, about eighty minutes. You will turn charts into code, code into charts, and — at the halfway point — start working on each other’s algorithms instead of your own. Nothing is saved and nothing is sent; the only record of this session is what ends up in your notebook.

——:——

The clock is optional. It counts down one station at a time and nags nobody.

Pairing. Put a confident student with a hesitant one and give the hesitant one the keyboard at stations 3 and 5. The confident one may only talk. It is uncomfortable for about four minutes and then it works.
What to collect. Only station 6, on paper. Everything else is practice and marking it would turn a working session into a test. If you want a quick temperature check, ask each pair to call out how many breaks they found at station 5 — it tells you instantly who is testing properly.
Nobody gets stranded. Stations 1 and 3 have a three-step hint ladder and then a Show the answer button — it unlocks after the third hint or after three failed runs, and it keeps their own attempt so they can put it back. Station 2 can place one box at a time or reveal the whole chart. Station 4 fills one trace cell on request. Tell them at the start that pressing these is allowed; otherwise the proud ones sit silently doing nothing for ten minutes, which is the worst outcome available.
If the room has no laptops. Stations 1, 2 and 6 work entirely on paper straight from this page on the projector. Station 4 works with two notebooks and no computer at all — one writes the algorithm, the other traces it, and they argue about who is right. That argument is the lesson.
STATION 1 SOLOabout 10 minutes

Read the chart, write the code

The chart is the specification. Write pseudocode that does exactly what it says — no more, no less — and the page will run it against real data to find out whether you read the chart correctly.

Rules of the station

Work left to right across the three tabs. Do not guess from the title: follow the arrows. When your code passes every test, write it into your notebook before you move on, because you will need it again at station 6.

If you get stuck: press I am stuck for a hint. There are three, and each one gives away a little more. After the third, a button appears that shows the whole answer — and you are allowed to press it. Read it, close it, and then write it again yourself from memory. That is worth far more than sitting looking at a blank screen for ten minutes.

STATION 2 SOLOTHEN PAPERabout 12 minutes

Read the code, build the chart

The other direction, which is the one the exam asks for far more often. The skeleton below is already wired up — the arrows, the branches and the loop-backs are drawn. Your job is to work out which statement belongs in which box.

Rules of the station

Drag each statement into the box where it belongs, or tap a statement and then tap a box. Get all of them right, then close the laptop and draw the whole chart on paper from memory. Mark your own drawing against the checklist underneath. The drawing is the bit that earns marks in June, not the dragging.

If you get stuck: Place one for me fills a single box and tells you why it goes there. Do that twice and a button appears that shows the whole finished chart — then clear it and rebuild it yourself.

STATEMENTS — DRAG INTO THE CHART

Now draw it on paper — then mark yourself

Six ticks. Be honest; nobody is looking.

Any tick you could not give yourself is a mark you would have lost. Redraw it once with that one thing fixed — it takes ninety seconds and it is the only way the habit sticks.
STATION 3 PAIRSabout 12 minutes

Three real briefs

No chart, no starter code, no hand-holding. A sentence of English, and you turn it into something that runs. This is what the question looks like in the exam.

Pair up now. Grab the person next to you — one laptop between the two of you. Decide right now who is A and who is B. You will keep this same partner through stations 3, 4 and 5, swapping who is A and who is B as you go, so settle it once and move on.

A Driver

Hands on the keyboard. You may only type what the navigator says. If you disagree, say so out loud — do not just silently type the better version.

B Navigator

No touching the keyboard. Decide the variables and the loop before any typing starts, and say which loop you chose and why. Swap roles at each new brief. Nudge us is yours to call — three hints, then the answer.

TEST RESULTS

Press Run the tests.
Navigator’s job, written down. Before the driver types a single character you should be able to say: what goes in, what comes out, which variables exist, and whether the repeating bit is a FOR or a WHILE. If you cannot say all four, you are not ready to type — and that is true in the exam room too.
STATION 4 PAIRS · SWAPabout 12 minutes

Trace your partner’s algorithm

This is the one that matters. You are about to desk-check something you did not write and cannot predict — which is exactly the situation in every trace-table question you will ever be given.

Same pair as station 3. No need to find anyone new — just decide which one of you writes first this time.

How this works

One of you types an algorithm into the box and chooses the input values. Press Lock it in and the page builds a blank trace table from it — the right number of rows, the right columns, all empty. Now swap seats. Your partner fills the table. The page knows the real answer because it runs the algorithm, so it will tell them precisely which cell is wrong and which line of the code it came from. Then swap back and do it again with something nastier.

Step 1 — the author writes it

Keep it between 4 and 12 lines. It must stop on its own. Make it interesting: a loop, a condition, a variable that changes in a way your partner will not see coming.

Step 2 — the partner traces it

Fill every white box. Grey values are carried over from the row above, so you do not have to retype them — but check them, because that is rule three.

Author’s rule. You are not allowed to watch over their shoulder and you are not allowed to say “no, that’s wrong”. If they get it wrong, the interesting question is which line misled them — and nine times out of ten it is a line you could have written more clearly.
STATION 5 PAIRS · A vs Babout 10 minutes

Break it

One of you writes an algorithm to a fixed specification. The other one tries to find an input that makes it do the wrong thing. Five minutes each way.

Same pair as before. This time A builds and B attacks — you will swap halfway through, so whoever was B last time is A first this time.

The specification — both of you read this

Read one mark. Print the grade: 80 or more → A, 70 to 79 → B, 60 to 69 → C, 50 to 59 → D, below 50 → F. If the mark is below 0 or above 100, print INVALID and nothing else. That is the whole contract. Anything the code does that disagrees with this paragraph is a break.

A Builder

Write it below. You are allowed to test it yourself first — in fact you should. Then hand over and say nothing.

B Breaker

You may not read the code while you attack it. Scroll it out of view. Type one input at a time and watch what comes back.

A — build it

B — break it

Type a mark, press Try. The page runs A’s code and compares what came out with what the specification says should have come out.

Breakers: open this only when you are out of ideas

Six families of input that break most first attempts. You do not get to see which one works — go and try them.

  • The numbers exactly on a boundary: 80, 70, 60, 50
  • The numbers one below a boundary: 79, 69, 59, 49
  • The two ends of the valid range: 0 and 100
  • Just outside the range: −1 and 101
  • A long way outside: −500 and 1000
  • Something that is not a whole number at all: 49.5, 79.9
Builders, before you feel bad: professional software ships with these bugs constantly. The lesson is not that you wrote it badly, it is that you cannot find these by reading your own code. Somebody else, or a test plan, has to go looking.
STATION 6 SOLOPAPER · SILENT15 minutes

Exam drill

Laptops closed. Two questions, fifteen minutes, written by hand exactly as you would in June. The model answers are behind a button and you do not press it until the fifteen minutes are gone.

Question 1

[10 marks]

A weather station records the temperature at noon for seven days.

Write an algorithm that reads the seven temperatures, then prints the highest temperature recorded and the average temperature for the week.

  • (a) Write it in pseudocode. [6]
  • (b) Draw the corresponding flowchart. [4]

Question 2

[9 marks]

Study the algorithm below.

  • (a) Complete a trace table for the algorithm. [5]
  • (b) State what the algorithm does. [2]
  • (c) State one value of n for which this algorithm would print nothing useful, and explain why. [2]

Before you leave

Three questions. Answer them to yourself honestly — they are worth more than the eighty minutes you just spent.

  1. Which direction was harder — chart to code, or code to chart? Whichever it was, that is the one to practise tonight, not the comfortable one.
  2. At station 4, where did your trace go wrong? If you skipped a row, you will skip it again in June unless you decide now to write every single one.
  3. At station 5, which input broke it? Nine times out of ten it was a boundary. Put that number in your notes and start every test plan with it from now on.
Homework that actually helps. Take the algorithm you wrote at station 3 and give it to somebody who is not in this class — a parent, a sibling, anyone. Ask them to follow it literally, doing nothing you did not write. Whatever they get wrong is an ambiguity you left in, and you will never write that kind of line again.
